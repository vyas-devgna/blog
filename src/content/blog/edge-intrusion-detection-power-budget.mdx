A small edge gateway has to inspect traffic while leaving enough CPU, memory and power for the services it protects. HERMES explores that constraint with a rule filter in front of a compact neural network, deployed on two Raspberry Pi 5 nodes.

Arpankumar G. Raval and I are the authors of [HERMES: Design and Deployment of a Hybrid AI/ML Network Security System on ARM Clusters for Edge Environments](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-032-22062-2_24). This article was revised against the full eight-page research manuscript, rather than relying only on the published abstract. Section and table references below refer to that manuscript. The published chapter is a separate version; its abstract and the manuscript do not agree on every number.

The useful story is the architecture and its measured trade-offs. The numerical discrepancies also deserve a clear explanation: reported results, arithmetic derived from tables, and unresolved reporting questions are different kinds of evidence.

## The pipeline: settle easy cases before inference

Section IV and Figure 1 describe two stages. Incoming traffic first passes through a curated rule filter based on Snort community signatures. Traffic the rules cannot classify conclusively goes to the learned detector. Known threats can trigger an immediate alert; anomalous traffic detected by the model triggers a second alert path.

The manuscript describes a three-layer fully connected DNN with 64, 32 and 16 neurons, ReLU activations and dropout. Training happens offline. The implementation reports full-integer quantisation through TensorFlow Lite, a model-size reduction from about 800 KB to 200 KB, and export to ONNX for inference through ONNX Runtime on ARM. Those are descriptions of the prototype, not a complete reproducible model specification: the feature schema, output layer, training configuration and conversion artefacts would also be needed to reproduce it.

The reason for putting rules first is practical. If an inexpensive check settles a case, it can avoid an inference. The model provides a second path for traffic without a matching signature. Whether that saves work depends on the traffic mix and how often rules can make a conclusive decision.

## What ran on the cluster

Section V describes two Raspberry Pi 5 Model B boards, each with a quad-core Cortex-A76 at 2.4 GHz and 8 GB RAM. The nodes ran Ubuntu Server 22.04, Docker containers and K3s, connected through a Gigabit Ethernet switch with port mirroring.

Each node hosted a Detection Engine, Data Aggregator and Model Manager. The detection code used C/C++, libpcap and a Hyperscan-based rule filter. Redis handled shared alerts and counters; the Python model manager distributed versioned model and rule updates using gRPC. The paper reports a 2 GB memory limit and a CPU limit for the detection container.

Power was sampled with a Keysight N6705C at 100 Hz. The manuscript reports an average inference time of about 50 microseconds per packet separately from end-to-end processing latency. These measurements describe different boundaries: inference time is not the complete capture, filtering, queueing and response time.

The threat model assumes trusted, hardened nodes, secure boot and encrypted model storage. Physical capture is outside the study. Those assumptions should be checked for a real deployment; the evaluation does not demonstrate that every installation satisfies them.

## How the evaluation was described

Section VI uses CIC-IDS2017 and UNSW-NB15 traffic, normalised header and flow features, and a time-based 70/15/15 training, validation and test split. It also describes a zero-day simulation that withheld attacks from the final 20% of the time span. The relationship between that holdout and the 70/15/15 split is not fully specified in the manuscript.

A Linux DPDK replay system drove traffic into the switch. The simulated scenarios included ARP spoofing, Nmap port scans, Hydra SSH brute force and a custom DNS tunnel. Payload and timing variations were used to exercise polymorphic behaviour. The baselines were Snort 2.9.19, Suricata 6.0.8 and the same DNN without a rule prefilter, running on the same hardware.

This is a benchmark and replay evaluation. Withheld attack classes and synthetic variants are useful tests of generalisation, but they do not establish that the system detects arbitrary future exploits in production.

## Detection results: keep the metrics separate

Table I reports the following binary benign-versus-attack results:

| System                  | Accuracy | Precision | Recall | F1    |
| ----------------------- | -------- | --------- | ------ | ----- |
| Snort                   | 84.7%    | 0.823     | 0.731  | 0.774 |
| Suricata                | 85.9%    | 0.841     | 0.748  | 0.792 |
| DNN without rule filter | 91.3%    | 0.897     | 0.892  | 0.894 |
| HERMES                  | 94.7%    | 0.932     | 0.921  | 0.926 |

Here, **94.7% is reported accuracy**, not an interchangeable name for recall or detection rate. Section VIII.B separately reports detecting 94.7% of held-out attack flows in its zero-day experiment. The coincidence of those numbers does not make the experiments or metrics identical.

Calculated from Table I, HERMES is 8.8 percentage points above Suricata and 3.4 percentage points above the DNN-only baseline in accuracy. The manuscript's prose calls the Suricata difference a 12.3% absolute improvement, which the table does not support. Figure 5 reports ROC AUC of approximately 0.99; without the underlying score arrays, it cannot be independently recalculated here.

### The confusion matrix needs reconciliation

Table III is labelled as the CIC-IDS2017 test confusion matrix:

| Actual class | Predicted benign | Predicted attack |
| ------------ | ---------------- | ---------------- |
| Benign       | 42,847           | 392              |
| Attack       | 1,203            | 38,921           |

Using the displayed counts, the false-positive rate is `392 / (42,847 + 392)`, or approximately **0.91%**. The surrounding manuscript prose says 0.8%, while the published Springer abstract says 0.9%.

The same matrix yields about 98.09% accuracy, 0.990 precision, 0.970 recall and 0.980 F1. These do not reproduce Table I's HERMES row. The matrix may represent a different subset or aggregation, but the manuscript does not provide enough detail to establish that relationship. Table I values remain reported summary results; the matrix-derived values are arithmetic on Table III, not replacement benchmark results.

This matters operationally. A false-positive rate applies to a defined set of benign decisions. It cannot be multiplied directly by the packet-throughput figure to obtain alerts per second when the evaluation unit and alert aggregation differ.

## Resource costs: compare like with like

Table II reports the following mixed-traffic resource measurements:

| System                  | CPU utilisation | RAM      | Latency | Power per node | Throughput |
| ----------------------- | --------------- | -------- | ------- | -------------- | ---------- |
| Snort                   | 23.4%           | 512 MB   | 1.2 ms  | 3.8 W          | 9,500 pps  |
| Suricata                | 31.7%           | 768 MB   | 1.8 ms  | 4.6 W          | 11,200 pps |
| DNN without rule filter | 45.2%           | 1,024 MB | 3.1 ms  | 5.4 W          | 12,800 pps |
| HERMES                  | 28.9%           | 640 MB   | 2.4 ms  | 4.2 W          | 15,000 pps |

HERMES uses about **22.2% less power than the DNN-only baseline** in that table. It uses about 10.5% more power than Snort. Its latency is higher than the rule-only baselines and about **22.6% lower than DNN-only** using the table's 3.1 ms comparator. The paper's 33% latency reduction comes from a separate load comparison in the Figure 3 discussion: approximately 2.8 ms versus 4.2 ms at 15,000 pps. These operating points should not be combined.

The abstract's claim of 67% less power than typical x86 solutions is not substantiated by an x86 comparison row in the supplied manuscript. The concrete comparison above is between systems on the ARM testbed. Two nodes drawing 4.2 W each imply about 8.4 W for the boards together; that arithmetic is not a measured whole-system total including the switch and replay host.

The methodology increases traffic until packet loss rises above 0.1%. The results report approximately 15,000 pps and growing queueing delay beyond the operating range; the discussion notes increased latency above 18,000 pps. Although the methods mention tail latency, the supplied results do not give a numerical p95 or p99 value.

## Robustness and the limits of the evidence

Section VIII.A reports FGSM and PGD feature-space attacks with perturbation budgets up to epsilon 0.1. Reported accuracy is about 89.3% for HERMES and 67.2% for DNN-only under that experiment. This tests the stated adversarial setup; it is not a guarantee against every protocol-valid evasion strategy.

The paper identifies model staleness, inter-node communication overhead, complex blended attacks and additional real-world encrypted traffic as areas requiring more work. It reports roughly 3% communication overhead. It also discusses differential privacy in the privacy section while describing federated learning as future work; the supplied manuscript does not give a privacy budget or a reproducible accounting procedure, so this article makes no quantified privacy guarantee.

The data-availability statement says code and configuration will be available on reasonable request after publication. The supplied manuscript does not include the raw evaluation logs, exact split manifests or model binaries needed to resolve the reporting discrepancies or rerun the benchmark.

## What to take from HERMES

The prototype shows a concrete way to put a cheap decision stage in front of learned inference on constrained ARM hardware. Table II makes the trade-off visible: more detection capacity than the rule-only baselines, with a higher cost than Snort and a lower cost than the DNN-only system in the reported workload.

Before using the figures for a deployment decision, reconcile the summary table with the confusion matrix, identify the exact evaluation unit and splits, measure alert volume at the intended traffic mix, and inspect tail latency under load. Those checks turn an interesting published result into evidence for a specific installation.
